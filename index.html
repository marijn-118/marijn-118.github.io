The Illusion of the Average Neighborhood

A look at San Francisco crime data shows why neighborhood averages can be misleading: most incidents are clustered in a few places, and some of the pattern comes from policing itself.

By s257688, s253129, s253154 | Social Data Analysis 2026, Assignment 2

When people look for a place to live, they usually want a simple answer: is this neighborhood safe or not? That makes sense, but averages can hide a lot. A district can look risky on paper even when most of its blocks are quiet.

To dig into that, I used more than two decades of incident reports from SF OpenData. The dataset includes time, category, and location, but it is still a record of police-reported incidents, not a perfect measure of every crime that happened. Once the data is mapped out, the picture gets clearer: crime is not spread evenly across the city. It tends to gather in specific corners, blocks, and time windows, which changes how we should think about policing and city planning.

1. Where the Crime Actually Shows Up

Larceny theft is the most common offense in San Francisco, but the city-wide average does not tell the full story. When the city is broken into 100m by 100m blocks, most of those blocks are quiet. A small number of places carry a huge share of the incidents, while everything around them looks very different.

That is the problem with relying on one average for an entire neighborhood. If a single hotspot pushes the numbers up, the whole area can be labeled dangerous even though most of it looks nothing like that. The statistic becomes easy to quote, but hard to trust.

2. When the Patterns Repeat

If crime is clustered in space, it is often clustered in time too. Some offenses seem to follow a clear rhythm, but that rhythm is not always about criminal behavior. In some cases, it is also shaped by when police are present and what they are able to record.

This heatmap makes one thing obvious: not every incident category behaves the same way. Some offenses are reported no matter what, while others only enter the dataset when police are in the right place at the right time. That means part of the pattern may reflect enforcement, not just crime itself.

3. How Data Can Feed Itself

This is where the risk starts. If a map is built mostly from enforcement-heavy incidents, it can end up showing where police spend time, not just where harm is happening.

If a predictive policing model learns from that kind of map, it can start to repeat the same pattern over and over. More officers in one place means more recorded incidents there, which makes the next map look even more concentrated. That is the kind of loop researchers have warned about: the model is not simply describing reality, it is helping shape the data that comes next.

Conclusion

Data visualization is useful, but it can also be misleading if we read it too quickly. Neighborhood averages miss how concentrated crime can be. They also miss the fact that some of the data reflects police activity as much as public activity. If we want to understand a city honestly, we have to look past the average and ask what the map is actually measuring.
