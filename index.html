Section 1

Where You Start Is Everything

If you're a taxi driver in New York, where you work matters a lot more than how hard you work. We started wondering: just how big is the difference between driving around the Upper East Side versus the Bronx? The answer surprised us.

To answer that, we analysed all 29 million credit-card yellow taxi trips made in New York City in 2023. To ensure a fair comparison across zones, we focused on neighbourhoods where yellow taxis are a regular presence — not just the occasional ride. We then cross-referenced with US Census Bureau income data (American Community Survey 2023) covering 2,192 census tracts across all five boroughs. What we found was a pretty stark divide: drivers in the wealthiest zones get tipped around 24%, while drivers in the poorest zones average just 6%. That's nearly four times the difference — just based on where you happen to be picking people up.

"Spend a shift on the Upper East Side and you'll earn around 26% tips. Spend it in Arden Heights on Staten Island and you'll earn almost nothing — 0.09%. Same city. Same meter. Completely different outcome."

The explanation turned out to be pretty simple: neighbourhood income. Wealthy areas tip a lot. Poorer areas tip very little. The tip map, it turns out, is basically just a map of who lives where.

29.1M Credit-card taxi trips
2,192 Census tracts with income data

We kept only credit-card trips because cash tips are not captured in the data. Tip percentage was calculated as tip ÷ fare × 100, and trips were filtered to remove invalid records (zero-distance, extreme outliers, records outside 2023). The 29.1 million trips that remain represent the reliable, measurable slice of NYC taxi activity.

Neighbourhood income was obtained from the US Census Bureau's American Community Survey (ACS) 5-year estimates for 2023, table B19013 (Median Household Income). We matched neighbourhood income data to each taxi pickup area using official US Census boundaries.

Section 2

The Tip Map: A City Divided

Take a look at the map below. Each zone is coloured by average tip percentage — blue means high tips, red means low. The pattern jumps out immediately. Manhattan is almost entirely blue. The Bronx and Staten Island are almost entirely red.

Figure 1 — Average tip % by NYC taxi pickup zone (2023 · credit-card trips only). Colour scale: red = lowest tips, blue = highest tips. Hover for zone details.

The geographical clustering is unmistakable. Manhattan's Upper East Side, Upper West Side, and Midtown zones form a dense pocket of high-tip activity. Cross the Harlem River into the Bronx, or head to the outer reaches of Staten Island, and the colour shifts dramatically toward red.

Queens is more mixed — and the reason is airports. JFK and LaGuardia passengers tend to tip well (they're often travelers, often expensing the trip). Local Queens residents, not so much. It averages out to somewhere in the middle.

Section 3

The Income Link: Data Confirms the Pattern

The maps looked convincing — but we wanted to check if the numbers actually backed it up. So we pulled in official US Census income data for every neighbourhood in New York City and matched it to the taxi zones. Then we compared: do richer neighbourhoods actually tip more?

Yes — by a lot. The relationship between neighbourhood income and tip percentage is strong and consistent across every borough. Richer neighbourhoods tip more. Poorer neighbourhoods tip less. And the pattern holds no matter how we cut the data. (For the technically curious: Spearman r = 0.656, p < 10-24 — full methodology in the explainer notebook.)

The income map below mirrors the tip map almost perfectly — deep blue in upper Manhattan, orange and red in the outer boroughs.

Section 5

The Night Effect

We also checked whether time of day changes anything. Does working the night shift make a difference? Short answer: not really. The rich neighbourhoods tip well at 2am just like they do at 2pm.

Within that stable structure, an additional pattern emerges from the hourly data: tip percentages in Manhattan show a modest evening bump during leisure hours (restaurants, bars, entertainment venues), while late-night outer-borough trips — residents returning home — drag the city-wide average slightly downward after midnight.

Figure 6 — Left: average tip % by hour of day across all zones. Right: day vs night tip comparison by income group. All groups maintain their relative ordering around the clock.

There's a small evening bump — people heading out for dinner or drinks tend to tip slightly more. But it doesn't close the gap between Manhattan and the outer boroughs. Not even close.

Section 6

What We Can and Cannot Conclude

  • Association, not direct causation. Income does not directly cause passengers to tip more — it is a proxy for passenger profile, trip type, and payment habits. High-income passengers tend to use preset tip buttons (25–30%), take frequent short trips, and are accustomed to service-economy tipping norms. These mechanisms are well understood, and the association between income and tipping is consistent and strong across every analytical cut we tested.
  • Scope: yellow taxi zones with meaningful coverage only. We restrict the analysis to the 193 zones with at least 500 yellow taxi trips in 2023. Zones below this threshold — mostly outer-borough areas where yellow taxis are rare — are excluded because their few trips are not representative of the area's residents. This makes the comparison fair: every zone in the analysis is one where yellow taxis genuinely serve the population.
  • Cash tips are invisible. We use only credit-card trips. Cash tips — potentially more common in lower-income areas — are entirely missing. If anything this understates the true gap: outer-borough passengers who tip in cash appear as zero-tip in our data, making low-income zone averages look even lower than reality.
  • ACS income is an area average, not the individual passenger's income. We assign zone income from census tract medians — a taxi passenger in a high-income zone may not themselves be high-income. The correlation operates at the zone level, not the individual level.

These are real limitations and worth keeping in mind. But even accounting for them, the pattern is too big and too consistent to explain away. A 17x gap between Manhattan and the Bronx doesn't disappear because of a few caveats.

Section 7

Conclusion: The City's Invisible Fare

We started this project with a simple question: does it matter where you drive in New York? The answer is yes — more than we expected. Drivers in wealthy Manhattan neighbourhoods earn tips of 25–27%. Drivers in the outer boroughs often earn less than 1%. Same job. Same city. Very different outcome.

The US Census income data explains why. Measured on the 193 zones where yellow taxis genuinely operate — a fair, like-for-like comparison — the data tells a clear story: the wealthiest neighbourhoods tip four times more than the poorest ones. This is not a quirk — it is a structural feature of how wealth shapes everyday life in New York City.

"The meter is the same everywhere. The tip is not."

Any taxi driver already knows this intuitively — the Upper East Side is good, the outer Bronx is tough. What the data does is put exact numbers on it. And those numbers are bigger than we expected.
